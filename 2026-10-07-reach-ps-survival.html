Before We Model

Design Comes First

Aki’s lecture covered the basics of study design and causal inference. In the next sections, we will introduce the basic statistical machinery used in OHDSI to execute OHDSI studies.

Before choosing a model, a study needs answers to several questions:

  • What is the research question?
  • Which groups are being compared?
  • What is the outcome?
  • Over what period of time will it be measured?

A statistical model cannot fix what is spoiled by design.

Directed Acyclic Graphs

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is a picture of the causal relationships we assume. Each box is a variable, and each arrow means “affects”. The graph is acyclic because no variable can loop back and cause itself.

  • A path from treatment to outcome that follows the arrows forward is the causal effect we want to estimate.
  • A backdoor path is any other path that connects treatment and outcome through an arrow pointing into treatment. Backdoor paths create associations between treatment and outcome that are not caused by the treatment.
  • A study estimates the causal effect once every backdoor path is blocked, either by design or by adjusting for the right variables.

Further Reading/Viewing on Design, Causal Inference, & DAGs

These are great resources on causal inference, design, and DAGS:

  • Morgan, S. L., and Winship, C. (2015). Counterfactuals and Causal Inference: Methods and Principles for Social Research (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press.
  • Pearl, J. (2009). Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press.
  • Pearl, J., Glymour, M., and Jewell, N. P. (2016). Causal Inference in Statistics: A Primer. Wiley.
  • Richard McElreath’s Statistical Rethinking course, whose lectures and materials are freely available online at github.com/rmcelreath.

An Example DAG

Suppose we compare celecoxib with a traditional NSAID and measure the risk of a GI bleed. Celecoxib is often chosen for patients with a history of ulcers, and those patients are also more likely to bleed.

The arrow from celecoxib to GI bleed is the effect we want. The following path is a backdoor path:

celecoxib ← prior ulcer → GI bleed

Unless we adjust for prior ulcers, it would make celecoxib look more harmful than it is.

Why Randomized Trials Work

  • In a randomized controlled trial, chance alone decides who receives the treatment.
  • As a result, the treated and untreated groups are expected to be the same at baseline in every respect, measured or not.
  • Any systematic difference in outcomes between the groups can therefore be attributed to the treatment.

The Structure of a Trial

In a trial, randomization is the only cause of treatment. Other causes of the outcome, such as age and sex, have no arrow into treatment.

Because nothing else points into treatment, the only path from treatment to outcome is the direct one, and a simple comparison of the two groups estimates the treatment effect.

Observational Data

In observational data, treatment is not assigned at random. Doctors and patients choose treatments for various reasons, from the state of evidence to limitations of insurance and the health system.

A confounder is a variable that affects both which treatment a person receives and the outcome.

The Structure of Confounding

In observational data, a confounder adds an arrow into treatment.

The confounder opens a backdoor path from treatment to outcome. Comparing treated and untreated patients without accounting for it mixes the two paths together, biasing the estimated effect size of the treatment.

Confounding by Age and Sex

In today’s simulations, older patients and men are more likely to be treated. They are also more likely to have worse outcomes.

A comparison of treated and untreated patients that ignores age and sex mixes the effect of the treatment with the effects of age and sex.

Different Research Outcomes, Different Models

Four Common Models

The right model depends on the type of outcome being studied.

Conditional Effects

Each treatment effect is conditional on the other covariates in the model. It compares treated and untreated patients who have the same values of those covariates. You will often read statements like “all else equal” or “controlling for” to convey how covariates are adjusted.

Learning With Simulation

  • In a simulation, we choose the true effect of the treatment before generating the data.
  • We can then check whether a method recovers that effect.
  • Testing a method on simulated data shows how it behaves before we trust it on real data, where the true effect is unknown.
  • I find this helps build understanding of how confounding actually works.

Two Simulated Datasets

We build two datasets of 10,000 patients each and reuse them for every model.

  • trial: a randomized trial. A coin flip decides treatment.
  • obs: observational data. Age and sex affect both treatment and the outcome.

For each model, we add an outcome with a known treatment effect to both datasets. We then check whether the model recovers that effect.

Linear Regression

Linear Regression (OLS)

Linear regression models a continuous outcome as a straight-line function of the predictors.

  • The intercept is the expected outcome when every predictor is zero.
  • The coefficient on treatment is the expected difference in the outcome between treated and untreated patients who have the same values of the other predictors.

In the simulation, the outcome is systolic blood pressure, and the treatment lowers it by 5 mmHg.

Simulating a Randomized Trial

set.seed(21)
n <- 10000

# A coin flip assigns treatment: 1 = treated, 0 = untreated
trial <- data.frame(treated = rbinom(n, size = 1, prob = 0.5))

# Blood pressure averages 140; treatment lowers it by 5; rnorm() adds noise
trial$sbp <- 140 - 5 * trial$treated + rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 10)

head(trial, 4)
  treated      sbp
1       1 121.4889
2       0 131.8583
3       1 137.8211
4       0 139.7353

Fitting the Model

lm() fits a linear regression. coef() returns the estimates, and confint() returns their 95% confidence intervals.

fit_ols_trial <- lm(sbp ~ treated, data = trial)
round(cbind(estimate = coef(fit_ols_trial), confint(fit_ols_trial)), 2)
            estimate  2.5 % 97.5 %
(Intercept)   140.19 139.91 140.47
treated        -5.13  -5.52  -4.74

The coefficient on treated is close to the true effect of −5. With a single binary predictor, it equals the difference between the two group means:

Logistic Regression

Logistic Regression

Logistic regression models a binary outcome, such as whether a patient has a GI bleed.

  • The odds of an event are the probability it happens divided by the probability it does not. A 20% risk is odds of 0.2 / 0.8 = 0.25.
  • The model estimates coefficients on the log odds scale.
  • Exponentiating a coefficient with exp() gives an odds ratio. An odds ratio of 2 means the treatment doubles the odds of the outcome.

In the simulation, the treatment doubles the odds of a GI bleed.

Logistic: Randomized Trial

rbinom() draws a 0 or 1 for each patient, using the probability from plogis().

trial$bleed <- rbinom(n, size = 1, prob = plogis(-2 + log(2) * trial$treated))

fit_logit_trial <- glm(bleed ~ treated, family = binomial, data = trial)
round(exp(cbind(odds_ratio = coef(fit_logit_trial), confint(fit_logit_trial))), 2)
            odds_ratio 2.5 % 97.5 %
(Intercept)       0.14  0.13   0.15
treated           1.96  1.76   2.19

The odds ratio for treated is close to the true value of 2. The intercept row is the odds of a bleed among untreated patients.

Logistic: Observational, Unadjusted

Older patients and men are also more likely to bleed.

obs$bleed <- rbinom(n, size = 1, prob = plogis(

Poisson Regression

Poisson Regression

Poisson regression models counts of events, such as the number of ER visits.

  • Patients are followed for different lengths of time. A patient followed for three years has more time to visit the ER than one followed for six months.
  • The model accounts for this with an offset: the log of each patient’s person-time.
  • The model then estimates a rate, which for us is often an incidence rate.
  • Exponentiating a coefficient gives a rate ratio.

In the simulation, the treatment raises the rate of ER visits by 50%, a rate ratio of 1.5.

Poisson: Randomized Trial

Each patient is followed for between six months and three years. rpois() draws a count of visits from that patient’s rate and follow-up time.

trial$years <- runif(n, min = 0.5, max = 3)
trial$visits <- rpois(n, lambda = 0.5 * 1.5^trial$treated * trial$years)

fit_pois_trial <- glm(visits ~ treated + offset(log(years)),
                      family = poisson, data = trial)
round(exp(cbind(rate_ratio = coef(fit_pois_trial), confint(fit_pois_trial))), 2)
            rate_ratio 2.5 % 97.5 %
(Intercept)       0.51  0.50   0.53
treated           1.48  1.42   1.53

The intercept row is the rate among untreated patients: about 0.5 visits per person-year.

Poisson: Observational, Unadjusted

obs$years <- runif(n, min = 0.5, max = 3)
obs$visits <- rpois(n, lambda = 0.5 * 1.5^obs$treated *
  exp(0.03 * (obs$age - 60) + 0.3 * obs$male) * obs$years)

fit_pois_naive <- glm(visits ~ treated + offset(log(years)),
                      family = poisson, data = obs)
round(exp(cbind(rate_ratio = coef(fit_pois_naive), confint(fit_pois_naive))), 2)
            rate_ratio 2.5 % 97.5 %
(Intercept)       0.55  0.54   0.56
treated           2.08  2.01   2.16

The unadjusted rate ratio is 2.08. The true value is 1.5.

Poisson: Observational, Adjusted

                    family = poisson, data = obs)
round(exp(cbind(rate_ratio = coef(fit_pois_adj), confint(fit_pois_adj))), 2)
            rate_ratio 2.5 % 97.5 %
(Intercept)       0.08  0.07   0.09
treated           1.51  1.45   1.57

Survival Analysis

Survival Analysis

Survival analysis models the time until an event occurs.

  • Some patients leave the data before the event can be observed, for example because they change insurance, switch systems, or die. This is called censoring. We know only that they had not had the event by the time they left.
  • Kaplan–Meier curves show the proportion of each group that is still event-free over time.
  • The Cox model estimates a hazard ratio: the ratio of the event rates in the two groups at any point in time.
  • The Cox model assumes that this ratio is constant over time. This is the proportional hazards assumption.

In the simulation, the treatment doubles the hazard of a GI bleed.

Data Requirements

Survival analysis needs one row per patient with at least three pieces of information.

  • A time zero, the date follow-up starts. In OHDSI studies this is usually the cohort start date, when the patient begins treatment.
  • A follow-up time, the time from time zero to the event or to the end of follow-up, whichever comes first.
  • An event indicator, which is 1 if the event was observed and 0 if the patient was censored.

To compute these, we need the date of the outcome and the date each patient’s follow-up ends, such as the end of their observation period. The Cox model also assumes that censoring is unrelated to the risk of the event once we account for the covariates.

Simulating Time to Event

rexp() draws each patient’s time to a GI bleed. A second draw sets when each patient leaves the data.

bleed_time <- rexp(n, rate = 0.1 * 2^trial$treated)  # years
censor_time <- runif(n, min = 0, max = 5)

# We observe whichever comes first
trial$time <- pmin(bleed_time, censor_time)
trial$event <- as.integer(bleed_time <= censor_time)  # 1 = bleed, 0 = censored

head(trial[, c("treated", "time", "event")], 4)
  treated     time event
1       1 1.454757     0
2       0 1.854889     0
3       1 4.374877     0
4       0 1.075882     1

Kaplan–Meier Curves

Surv() combines the follow-up time and the event indicator. survfit() estimates the curves.

km <- survfit(Surv(time, event) ~ treated, data = trial)
km_data <- data.frame(time = km$time, surv = km$surv,
                      group = rep(c("Untreated", "Treated"), km$strata))

ggplot(km_data, aes(x = time, y = surv, color = group)) +
  geom_step(linewidth = 1.5) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c(Untreated = "#7E7E7C", Treated = "#002D72"), name = NULL) +
  labs(x = "Years since treatment start", y = "Proportion without a GI bleed")

coxph() fits the Cox model. Its output has no intercept.

round(exp(cbind(hazard_ratio = coef(fit_cox_trial), confint(fit_cox_trial))), 2)
        hazard_ratio 2.5 % 97.5 %
treated          2.1  1.95   2.27

The hazard ratio is close to the true value of 2.

From Two Confounders to Thousands

The Problem With Real Data

In the simulations, there were two confounders, and we knew what they were.

In OMOP data, each patient can have thousands of recorded conditions, drugs, procedures, and measurements. Any of them might be a confounder. Adding thousands of covariates to an outcome model is difficult to do well.

Propensity Scores: A Preview

A propensity score is the probability that a patient receives the treatment, given their covariates.

  • Patients with similar propensity scores have similar covariates, on average.
  • Matching treated and untreated patients on the score produces groups that are comparable at baseline, as in a trial.
  • The outcome model can then compare the matched groups directly, without adding the covariates.

What Are We Estimating?

The estimand is the quantity a study sets out to estimate. For a treatment effect, the main question is: the effect in whom?

  • ATE, the average treatment effect: the effect if everyone were treated versus no one
  • ATT, the average treatment effect in the treated: the effect among patients who actually received the treatment
  • ATC, the average treatment effect in the controls: the effect among patients who did not receive it

If the treatment works the same for everyone, the three are equal. When the effect differs across patients, and the treated differ from the untreated, they are not.

Estimands and Propensity Scores

The estimand follows from how the propensity score is used.

  • Matching each treated patient to an untreated patient estimates the ATT. This is the matchit() default.
  • Matching each untreated patient to a treated patient estimates the ATC (estimand = "ATC").
  • Weighting the whole sample, or full matching, can estimate the ATE (estimand = "ATE").

A caliper drops treated patients with no close match. The estimate then applies to the treated patients who could be matched, which can differ from all treated patients.

Regression Hides Its Estimand

  • An outcome model like lm(sbp ~ treated + age + male) returns one coefficient for treatment.
  • If the effect differs across patients, that coefficient is a weighted average of the effect across covariate groups. It gives the most weight to groups where treatment is close to 50/50, which are not the treated, the untreated, or the whole population.
  • This is true even when the model adjusts for every confounder.

Regression can target the ATE or ATT: fit the outcome model, predict each patient’s outcome treated and untreated, and average over the chosen population. That step has to be done on purpose. With propensity scores, the choice is part of the design.

Matching on the Propensity Score

matchit() estimates each patient’s propensity score from age and sex. It then pairs each treated patient with the untreated patient whose score is closest, so the estimand is the ATT. The caliper sets how close two scores must be. Treated patients with no untreated patient within the caliper are dropped.

matched <- matchit(treated ~ age + male, data = obs, caliper = 0.05)
matched_obs <- match.data(matched)

Doubly Robust Estimation

We now have two ways to handle confounding:

  • A propensity score model, which predicts treatment from the covariates
  • An outcome model, which predicts the outcome from treatment and the covariates

Either one gives a biased answer if its model is wrong.

A doubly robust approach uses both. Its estimate stays unbiased if either model is correct, so the analysis gets two chances to get it right.

A Misspecified Propensity Score

Suppose we leave sex out of the propensity score model.

matched_bad <- matchit(treated ~ age, data = obs, caliper = 0.05)
matched_bad_obs <- match.data(matched_bad)

aggregate(cbind(age, male) ~ treated, data = matched_bad_obs, FUN = mean)
  treated      age      male
1       0 63.76253 0.4169649
2       1 63.86735 0.6481747

Age is balanced, but the treated group still has more men.

The Outcome Model With the Wrong Score

With only the treatment in the outcome model, the leftover imbalance in sex biases the estimate.

fit_ps_bad <- lm(sbp ~ treated, data = matched_bad_obs)
round(cbind(estimate = coef(fit_ps_bad), confint(fit_ps_bad)), 2)
            estimate  2.5 % 97.5 %
(Intercept)   146.30 145.87 146.73
treated        -3.98  -4.59  -3.38

The estimate is −4.0 mmHg. The true effect is −5.

Matching Plus Covariate Adjustment

Now we keep the same flawed matches, but adjust for age and sex in the outcome model.

fit_dr <- lm(sbp ~ treated + age + male, data = matched_bad_obs)
round(cbind(estimate = coef(fit_dr), confint(fit_dr)), 2)
            estimate 2.5 % 97.5 %
(Intercept)    99.81 97.69 101.92
treated        -4.91 -5.45  -4.37
age             0.70  0.67   0.74
male            3.70  3.16   4.24

The estimate is −4.9 mmHg. The propensity score model was wrong, but the outcome model was right, so the estimate is still close to −5.

Many Covariates, Few Patients

  • In OMOP data, every condition, drug, procedure, and measurement code can become a covariate. CohortMethod routinely builds tens of thousands of them.
  • Many codes record the same underlying fact. A diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, a metformin prescription, and an HbA1c test all point to diabetes.
  • For a rare exposure or a small database, there can be fewer patients than covariates.
  • Ordinary logistic regression cannot estimate a propensity score in that setting.

Simulating High-Dimensional Data

This data set has 500 patients and 1,000 binary codes. Each of 20 underlying conditions is recorded by 50 related codes. Three conditions drive both treatment and blood pressure. The true effect is again −5.

set.seed(7)
n_patients <- 500
n_conditions <- 20
codes_per_condition <- 50

condition <- matrix(rbinom(n_patients * n_conditions, size = 1, prob = 0.25), ncol = n_conditions)

# A patient with the condition usually has each related code; others rarely do
codes <- lapply(seq_len(n_conditions), function(k) {
  prob <- ifelse(condition[, k] == 1, 0.6, 0.02)
  matrix(rbinom(n_patients * codes_per_condition, size = 1, prob = prob), ncol = codes_per_condition)
})
codes <- do.call(cbind, codes)
colnames(codes) <- sprintf("code_%04d", seq_len(ncol(codes)))

treated <- rbinom(n_patients, size = 1,
                  prob = plogis(-1 + 1.5 * condition[, 1] + 1.5 * condition[, 2] - condition[, 3]))
sbp <- 130 - 5 * treated + 8 * condition[, 1] + 6 * condition[, 2] - 4 * condition[, 3] +
  rnorm(n_patients, sd = 10)

hd <- data.frame(treated, sbp, codes)
ps_formula <- reformulate(colnames(codes), response = "treated")

A Standard Propensity Score Fails

fit_ps_glm <- glm(ps_formula, family = binomial, data = hd)

# Coefficients that could not be estimated
sum(is.na(coef(fit_ps_glm)))
[1] 501
# Estimated propensity scores
round(range(fitted(fit_ps_glm)), 3)
[1] 0 1

With more covariates than patients, the model cannot estimate half its coefficients, and the scores it does produce are pushed to 0 and 1. These scores cannot be used to match patients. matchit() fits this same model by default.

The Lasso

  • The lasso is logistic regression with a penalty on the size of the coefficients.
  • The penalty shrinks most coefficients to exactly zero. The model keeps only the covariates that help predict treatment.
  • Cross-validation picks how strong the penalty should be.
  • Among a cluster of related codes, the lasso tends to keep a few and drop the rest.
  • CohortMethod fits its propensity scores this way, using a regularized regression package called Cyclops.
